## 3. What reversal costs

The residue tape is the only thing kept in order to go backwards: one
integer per contact event, nothing per frame and nothing per body. The
snapshot column is what storing every frame would cost instead, using the
same varint encoding for both sides, so the two are comparable.

| scene | steps | contacts | tape entries | tape bytes | bits per contact | snapshot bytes | ratio |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| drop | 10000 | 5579 | 5579 | 15843 | 22.7 | 840000 | 53x |
| billiards | 10000 | 203 | 203 | 588 | 23.2 | 600000 | 1020x |
| scatter | 10000 | 21264 | 21264 | 58861 | 22.1 | 1440000 | 24x |
| single | 10000 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0.0 | 100000 | n/a |

The `single` row is the null control: one ball cannot contact anything, so
the tape stays at zero bytes and the rewind is genuinely free.

## 4. Controls

**Determinism.** Two independent runs of the same scene, 5,000 steps: states
identical = True, residue tapes identical = True.

**Energy, elastic scene.** With no gravity the kinetic energy is the whole
energy budget, so it should stay put. Over 10,000 steps: 67092521 -> 67120671, a change
of 0.0420%. The residual is the integer rounding in contact resolution, which
is exactly the information the tape records.

**Momentum across contact.** The impulse is applied equal and opposite as
integers, so momentum is conserved to the last bit: (656, 131) -> (656, 131), with the
velocities genuinely changed by the contact: True.

**Wall rule, exhaustive.** Every state on a 41-position axis with every
velocity the rule accepts: 3321 states, 3321 distinct images, 0 collisions,
3321 of 3321 inverted exactly.

**Wall rule without the half-unit offset**, the same enumeration over a
smaller velocity range: 697 states, 16 of them landing on a state another
state also reaches. That is why the offset is there: without it the step is
not a bijection and no amount of bookkeeping recovers the lost state.

## 4b. How much of the contact activity is the same pair, over and over

Milestone 1 resolves every overlapping pair on every step. A pair that stays
overlapped therefore has its relative normal velocity exchanged repeatedly,
which is reversible but is not how contact should behave. This measures how
often that happens, so the limitation is a number rather than a hedge.

| scene | steps | contact events | pairs newly overlapping | repeats of a pair already overlapping | repeat share |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| drop | 5000 | 1911 | 622 | 1289 | 67.5% |
| billiards | 5000 | 105 | 105 | 0 | 0.0% |
| scatter | 5000 | 14013 | 1121 | 12892 | 92.0% |
| single | 5000 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0.0% |

Milestone 2 replaces this rule with one that keeps bodies from overlapping at
all, which is both better contact physics and, being a reflection in the
relative coordinate, the same trick that already makes the walls reversible.

## 6. What this run does not show

- No rotation, no polygons, no friction, no restitution: milestone 1 is discs
  with elastic contact. Those milestones are listed in `TASKS.json`.
- Bodies that stay overlapped for several steps have their relative normal
  velocity exchanged on every one of those steps. It is reversible, and it is
  not good contact physics. See the status section of the README.
- The comparison against snapshots is a storage comparison only. No claim is
  made here about speed against any other engine.
